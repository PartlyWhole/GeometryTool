# What makes an item high quality

This module teaches segments, angles and two-column proof to high-school
students, modelled on a real test paper. Its central claim — the one every
other lesson leans on — is that **only what a figure marks may be used**, never
what it looks like. Items are judged against that purpose, not against general
notions of polish.

An item is judged on seven criteria. Each is a question with a yes/no answer
and a named failure mode. An item does not have to excel on all seven; most
criteria are simply "no problem here", and saying so is the expected result.

### 1. Correct
*Is it mathematically right, and does exactly one offered answer satisfy it?*

Failures: two options are defensible; the keyed answer is wrong; the figure's
geometry contradicts the item's text; an accepted-answer list omits a form the
student will legitimately produce. **A correctness failure is a bug, not a
quality judgement — flag it separately and always.**

### 2. Tests what it claims
*Can a student who understands the concept fail it, and can a student who does
not understand it pass?*

Failures — the item is passable by a shortcut that bypasses the concept:
- the answer is readable off the figure by eye, or off a printed numeral
- one option is the only one of its grammatical or mathematical type
- elimination works without knowing the concept
- the prompt states the fact the student was supposed to extract
- the conclusion is visible before the reasoning starts

This is the criterion most items fail on, and it is the most important one.

### 3. Distractors earn their place
*Does each wrong option correspond to a specific misconception you could name?*

Failures: a dead option nobody picks; two distractors testing the same slip;
an option wrong only on a technicality the student cannot see.

Not applicable to open-response items; say so rather than forcing it.

### 4. The figure carries its weight
*Does the figure mark what the item needs, and does it invite only readings it
supports?*

Failures: the item asserts a fact the figure does not mark; the figure is
decorative and the prose would do alone; the drawing invites an unmarked
reading the item does not then address; a figure is absent where one would
carry the argument. A figure that deliberately looks misleading while marking
honestly is a strength here, not a fault — that is the module's whole lesson.

### 5. The explanation answers the error
*Does the feedback address the mistake the student actually made?*

Failures: a generic note about the concept attached regardless of what was
asked; a restatement of the answer; an explanation naming things absent from
the item; no explanation where a specific trap was laid.

### 6. Scaffolding stages the work
*Do the hints let a student get unstuck without handing over the answer?*

Failures: the first hint is the whole solution; hints taken together are a
complete walkthrough with nothing left; a hint addresses the easy half while
the hard half goes unsupported; no hint where the item has a single make-or-
break insight.

Not applicable where the format has no hints; say so.

### 7. It earns its slot
*Does it add something no sibling item already does, and does it belong here?*

Failures: a near-duplicate of a neighbour; a concept covered better elsewhere;
content outside the module's scope (segments, angles, proof) that asks the
student to supply a fact from another course.

---

## Verdict

One per item, chosen for what it implies about the work:

- **Keep** — works as intended. Minor observations may still be noted.
- **Tweak** — sound, with one specific cheap change named (a reworded prompt, a
  swapped distractor, an added accepted form, a declared trap).
- **Rework** — the underlying idea is worth teaching, but as built the item does
  not test it. Say what it would take.
- **Cut** — it does not earn its slot, and the concept is served elsewhere.

Plus, independently of the verdict: **BUG** where criterion 1 fails. A bug is a
correctness defect and must be fixed regardless of whether the item is kept.

## Calibration

Expect a spread. Content this heavily revised should land mostly on Keep and
Tweak, with Rework uncommon and Cut rare. If you find yourself marking most of a
batch Rework, your bar has drifted — re-read criterion 2 and ask whether the
item genuinely fails it or merely could be sharper. "Could be sharper" is not a
defect. Equally, do not soften a real failure into a Tweak to keep the
distribution comfortable.

Judge the item as built, for the student it is aimed at. An item that is easy is
not thereby bad: a confidence item, an on-ramp that teaches an interface, and a
recall check each have a job. Ask whether it does its job, not whether it is
hard.
